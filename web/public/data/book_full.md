Actually, nine of the last 12 quarters. “We’re running very hard, reaching nowhere,” he whispered to himself. There is a foundational assumption in management that most people have not questioned. It just felt normal. It still does, to most leaders running organizations today. 

Here it is, the Blind Spot of the Management World: 

The job of management is to use resources smartly to get maximum output. 

Strip away every framework produced in a hundred years of management thinking. This assumption is at the heart of most of them. Treated as obvious. 

The following pages ask you to question it. 

#### The Extractive Century 

For the better part of a century, management has operated on one foundational mindset: Extract Maximum.

Land, Capital, Technology, Machinery, Raw Material, People, Nature, Everything is a Resource. 

We call people at work ‘Human Resources’, not sources. Resources to be allocated, used, and replaced. 

On the surface, it appears to work. Output surges. Metrics improve. Shareholders are satisfied. Beneath that surface, however, the ground is hollowing. 

The hollowing shows up first in the people. Energy quietly withdraws. Contribution narrows. Somewhere, in the privacy of their own experience, each person makes a critical decision: 

“Go through the motions. Make the right noises. Don’t bring your heart into the game. Protect yourself. Survive.” 

Then the culture cracks, followed by performance stagnation. 

People also start treating the company as a resource to full their goals, nothing more.

### The BUSYness Mirror 

#### Are you building a High-Performance Organization Or a BUSY one? 

Most leaders know something is wrong before they can name it. You end a week having attended twelve meetings, replied to hundreds of messages, resolved three escalations, and still can’t quite name what actually moved forward. 

BUSYness gives the illusion of productivity: calendars full and people in action. This is not random. BUSYness follows a predictable cycle, one that repeats until someone has the clarity and the courage to break it. 

It begins with Unclarity, an unclear purpose, strategy, or set of priorities. Without clarity, people default to Overactivity: reacting to everything that feels important because they have no filter to separate what is important from what is merely urgent. That overactivity spreads the organization thin; time, 

9 

Introduction: Reinventing Management 

energy, and resources are diluted across too many things simultaneously. The impact is shallow results: a lot of motion but little meaningful progress. That gap between effort and outcome produces frustration, exhaustion, dissatisfaction, and a creeping sense that the work is not worth what it costs. And what do leaders and teams reach for when frustrated and uncomfortable? More activity. More BUSYness. The cycle starts again.

The BUSYness Mirror is the simple act of standing back and looking at your organization as it actually is. It means understanding how clients see your organization and how your best people experience it. 

When you’re moving a lot, stopping to ask whether you’re moving in the right direction feels like a luxury you don’t have. 

##### 1. Blind Spots of the BUSY organization 

BUSYness rarely announces itself. Watch for these signs: 

#### 1.a Meetings run on visibility, not decisions. 

People sit in rooms for hours, giving updates, presenting, discussing, arguing, managing egos, and surviving. At the end of those hours, though, the question, “What did we impact?” becomes an uncomfortable question; the meeting felt productive, but produced no concrete decisions and no clear actions. 

In a BUSY organization, meetings consume significant time while generating few decisions that justify their cost. 

#### 1.b Follow-up is the operating rhythm. 

In our research across client organizations, senior leaders spend eight to twelve hours every week chasing updates, clarifying missed commitments, and extracting status across email, WhatsApp, Slack, calls, and meetings. The follow-up 

11

message has become such a normal part of the workflow that nobody questions it anymore. 

According to one HBR study (2017), managers spend 5.8 hours every week in unnecessary meetings. 

Across organizations, 53% of working time is now spent on what’s called “busywork”, activity that feels productive but generates no meaningful output (Asana, 2024). 

In one of our in-house sessions in a Delhi-HQ organization, the conference room held thirty leaders: the CEO, department heads, and senior managers of a 20,000- person company. All perpetually busy. We put one question in front of them: “What percentage of your time is spent on internal follow-ups?” 

The responses were shocking. The average response: 60– 70% of the entire organization’s time is spent chasing work. Not creating value, but managing the failure to create value on the first commitment. 

If your organization needs follow-up to move, you have Dangerous BUSYness Syndrome. 

#### 1.c Standards dropped without warning 

Slowly, one compromise at a time, under genuine pressure from real circumstances, the bar has dropped. The worst part is that most people in the organization don’t even realise the bar has moved. The new lower standard has simply become normal.

#### 1.d People just come to work, not to fulfil a Purpose. 

There is a difference, and you can feel it. When people are part of a real purpose, a cause they believe in, they show up differently. There is a special energy of being engrossed in creating a future. When that’s absent, people are professionally competent and spiritually hollow. They are doing a job, and they will do it until a better opportunity arrives. 

#### 1.e The same problems keep coming back. 

You solve a problem in Q1; it reappears in Q3 in a slightly different form. You address a team conflict; the same conflict is back three months later with different people. 

A BUSY organization is very good at treating symptoms and very poor at addressing the systems that keep producing them.

#### Strategy is Business Everything else is BUSYness. 

As strategy scholar Roger Martin argues, most organizations don’t have a strategy. They have a plan. A well-constructed, overly discussed plan with targets, timelines, and everything else. Strategy is a set of interconnected choices, and a real choice costs you something, by definition.
